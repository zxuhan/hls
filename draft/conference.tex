\begin{abstract}
Planning a downtime upgrade for a complex lithography machine
requires scheduling tens to hundreds of interdependent tasks under
tight resource and physical constraints. We formalise one such
problem, arising at ASML, a manufacturer of semiconductor lithography
machines, as a Resource-Constrained Project Scheduling Problem
(RCPSP) with five domain-specific extensions to the classical
formulation: single-day non-preemption, time-varying staffing capacity, 
spatial exclusivity, tool exclusivity, and per-axis
machine-configuration compatibility. Three algorithmic candidates are
evaluated on four real industrial instances: a priority-rule greedy
constructor with Most Total Successors (MTS) and Shortest Processing
Time (SPT) variants, an exact CP-SAT solver,
and a domain-enhanced greedy heuristic whose composite score
combines a critical-path remaining term with a continuous
machine-configuration affinity term and an optional
constraint-tightness term. The recommended heuristic configuration
attains a 6.1\% mean optimality gap to the CP-SAT incumbent across
the four instances, well below the pre-registered 15\% mean-gap
threshold, and represents a 37\% relative reduction in mean
optimality gap over the best classical priority rule, all at
sub-second cost. CP-SAT proves optimality in seconds on three of the four
instances but exhausts a 600-second budget without closing the gap
on a fourth. A CP-SAT budget sweep reveals complementary regimes:
in the sub-second window relevant for interactive scenario
iteration, CP-SAT returns no feasible solution on three of the four
instances and the heuristic is the only viable choice; from one
second of compute upward, CP-SAT is never worse than the heuristic
on any instance, with one-day wins on one instance at one second
and on three instances from ten seconds.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

ASML is a manufacturer of semiconductor lithography machines.
Industrial engineers there currently schedule the machine upgrade
process manually. The resulting schedule is referred to internally as
a high-level sequence. The process is slow, error-prone, and does not
scale as machines grow more complex. Schedule quality determines how
long the machine is out of service, which in semiconductor production
is the dominant economic cost. This paper investigates algorithms to
replace this manual process.

The core difficulty is that, beyond classical precedence, the
scheduler must simultaneously satisfy single-day non-preemption,
staffing capacity across shift windows, exclusive access to physical
workspace zones, exclusive access to specialised tools, and
machine-state compatibility (each task declares a value on a small set
of states, and concurrent tasks must agree on every state they both
declare). This combination positions the problem as a
Resource-Constrained Project Scheduling Problem
(RCPSP)~\cite{brucker1999rcpsp} with five domain-specific extensions.

RCPSP is NP-hard, and decades of research have produced two broad
solution families: exact solvers that prove optimality but whose cost
grows sharply with instance size and constraint interaction, and
faster heuristic constructions that trade optimality guarantees for
speed. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews both families and positions
the present formulation against them.

Two industrial considerations motivate studying heuristic alternatives
alongside the exact solver. First, scalability is uncertain: as ASML
machines grow more complex, it is not known whether an exact solver
scales well across instances of increasing size under all five
extension constraints. Second, speed matters in production: an engineer
often sweeps many planning variants within a single session, so a fast
heuristic with a small loss in solution quality can be preferable to a
slow exact solver even when the latter returns the optimum.

\subsection{Research question and hypotheses}
\label{sec:rqs}

This thesis addresses the following research question through a
comparative algorithmic study.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{RQ.} \emph{To what extent can a domain-enhanced
greedy heuristic close the optimality gap between classical
priority-rule constructive heuristics and the CP-SAT
constraint-programming solver in Google OR-Tools across real
industrial instances, at sub-second computational cost?}
\smallskip

Three candidates are evaluated: (A) a baseline greedy constructor
using standard RCPSP priority rules, (B) Google OR-Tools CP-SAT as an
exact benchmark, and (C) a domain-enhanced greedy heuristic (DEH) whose
composite scoring function encodes problem-specific constraints
directly into task selection. Two falsifiable hypotheses about the
heuristic are pre-registered, then evaluated on four real industrial
instances drawn from distinct machine modules.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{H1.} Over the four evaluated instances, the mean
  optimality gap of the best-configured domain-enhanced greedy
  heuristic against the CP-SAT solver is strictly smaller than
  that of both classical priority rules.
  \item \textbf{H2.} Over the four evaluated instances, the mean
  optimality gap of the best-configured domain-enhanced greedy
  heuristic against the CP-SAT solver does not exceed
  15\%.\footnote{Kolisch and Hartmann~\cite{kolisch2006experimental}
  report 10--14\% mean gaps for the best classical priority rules on
  PSPLIB; 15\% sits just above that band.}
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Resource-Constrained Project Scheduling}

In the classical RCPSP a set of tasks with fixed durations must be
scheduled subject to precedence constraints and a limited supply of
renewable resources, so as to minimise the makespan. The problem
generalises job-shop scheduling and is NP-hard in the strong
sense~\cite{blazewicz1983scheduling}. Standard notation, a
classification scheme, and a catalogue of models and methods are given
by Brucker et al.~\cite{brucker1999rcpsp}, with a book-length treatment
in the research handbook of Demeulemeester and
Herroelen~\cite{demeulemeester2002project}. Most empirical work in the
field is benchmarked on PSPLIB~\cite{kolisch1997psplib}, a library of
generated instances with known or best-known makespans, which has
shaped how heuristics are compared. The instances studied here are real
industrial machine modules at ASML rather than instances drawn from
PSPLIB, so these library baselines do not apply here.

\subsection{Exact Methods}

Exact approaches solve the problem to proven optimality. Early work used
dedicated branch-and-bound, such as the procedure of Stinson et
al.~\cite{stinson1978multiple} for the multiple-resource case.
Mixed-integer programming and constraint programming are now the
standard exact tools. Constraint-based scheduling, surveyed by
Baptiste et al.~\cite{Baptiste2001}, models each task as an interval
variable and reasons over global constraints such as \emph{cumulative}
for renewable resources and \emph{no-overlap} for disjunctive ones.
Modern CP solvers such as IBM ILOG CP Optimizer~\cite{laborie2018ibm}
and Google OR-Tools CP-SAT~\cite{ortools} pair these constraints with
strong filtering and systematic search. The cumulative constraint in
particular depends on specialised filtering, of which the edge-finding
rule of Vil\'im~\cite{vilim2015fastcumulative} and the conditional
time-interval reasoning of Laborie and
Rogerie~\cite{laborie2008complete} are representative. Such solvers
prove optimality on small and moderate instances, but their cost grows
quickly with size and constraint interaction, which is the behaviour we
probe directly in the budget sweep of Section~\ref{sec:budget-sweep}.

\subsection{Constructive and Metaheuristic Methods}

For larger or time-critical instances, heuristics remain the practical
standard. The simplest are priority-rule methods built on a
schedule-generation scheme: tasks are placed one at a time in an order
set by a priority rule, with the serial and parallel schemes differing
in how the next task is chosen. Kolisch~\cite{kolisch1996efficient}
studies the efficiency of common priority rules, among them the
most-total-successors and shortest-processing-time rules that we adopt
as the two baselines of Candidate A. Metaheuristics such as genetic
algorithms, tabu search, and simulated annealing then search the space
of activity orderings; the experimental survey of Kolisch and
Hartmann~\cite{kolisch2006experimental} compares many of these on
PSPLIB and reports mean optimality gaps of roughly 10 to 14\% for the
best priority rules. Hartmann and Briskorn~\cite{hartmann2010survey}
survey the broader space of variants and extensions. Our heuristic
stays in this cheap constructive class rather than the metaheuristic
one, since the target is fast, lightweight scheduling rather than the
longer search a metaheuristic performs.

\subsection{Industrial Project Scheduling Applications}

Beyond benchmark studies, RCPSP models and their extensions have been
applied to industrial planning problems that share structural features
with the present one. Maintenance turnaround and shutdown projects, in
which a facility is taken offline and a large set of interdependent jobs
must be completed under crew and equipment limits before it returns to
service, have been modelled as resource-constrained project
scheduling~\cite{megow2011turnaround}. Scheduling in
semiconductor manufacturing has likewise drawn sustained attention, for
both production and equipment operations, under tool-availability and
machine-configuration constraints~\cite{monch2011semiconductor}.
In these settings the planning objective is typically to minimise the
downtime or completion window, the direct analogue of the makespan
minimised here, which is what makes RCPSP the natural modelling frame
for the ASML upgrade-scheduling problem.

\subsection{Extensions and Positioning}

Real problems rarely match the textbook RCPSP, and a large literature
studies extensions: time-varying resource availability, multiple
execution modes, generalised precedence, and non-renewable or
partially renewable resources, all catalogued
in~\cite{hartmann2010survey}. Each constraint we impose has appeared
individually in prior work. Spatial and tool exclusivity are
disjunctive (no-overlap) resources, time-varying staffing is a calendar
on a renewable resource, and machine-state compatibility resembles a
sequence-dependent setup. What we have not found in the published
literature is this particular combination treated as a single
formulation, in particular machine-state compatibility together with
time-varying staffing capacity. Because instance structure drives both
solver difficulty and heuristic quality, and because generated
benchmarks need not reflect real structure~\cite{vanhoucke2008measurement},
we evaluate on real modules chosen to span complementary difficulty
profiles rather than on a single synthetic family.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Problem Formalisation}

The upgrade scheduling problem is a Resource-Constrained Project
Scheduling Problem (RCPSP) with five domain-specific extensions to
the classical formulation. In classical RCPSP, a set of tasks must
be scheduled subject to precedence and renewable resource
constraints, minimising total project duration (makespan). The
formulation here adds single-day non-preemption, time-varying
staffing capacity, spatial exclusivity, tool exclusivity, and per-axis
machine-configuration compatibility.

\subsubsection{Inputs}

The algorithm receives a \emph{shift schedule} defining the available
working time and a \emph{task set} $T$ defining the work to be
scheduled. A shift schedule is an ordered sequence of working days.
Each day contains one shift, specified by a start hour, an integer
duration in hours, and an integer staff capacity (the number of
engineers available throughout the shift).

Each task $i \in T$ declares an integer duration $d_i$ in hours; a
staff requirement $f_i$ (the number of engineers required throughout);
a value $p_i^{(a)}$ on each of eight independent machine-configuration
\emph{axes} $a$, with $p_i^{(a)} = \emptyset$ if the task imposes no
requirement on axis $a$; a set of workspace zones; and an optional
required tool, classified as either \emph{single-availability} (only
one instance exists, so tasks requiring it cannot overlap) or
\emph{multi-availability} (treated as effectively unlimited, imposing
no overlap constraint). A precedence partial order over $T$ is
supplied as a Boolean adjacency matrix encoding a directed acyclic
graph $(T, E)$, where $E$ is the set of precedence edges.

\subsubsection{Constraints}

Every algorithm enforces six hard constraints. A schedule is feasible
only if all six hold for every pair of placed tasks.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Single-day non-preemption.} Each task executes as a
  single uninterrupted interval inside one day, entirely within the
  shift hours of that day.
  \item \textbf{Time-varying staffing capacity.} At every hour within
  a shift, the sum of staff requirements across concurrent tasks
  must not exceed the shift's staff capacity (one staff unit is one
  engineer working that shift).
  \item \textbf{Spatial exclusivity.} Each task occupies a (possibly
  empty) set of workspace zones. Two tasks whose zone sets intersect
  may not execute concurrently.
  \item \textbf{Tool exclusivity.} No two tasks requiring the same
  single-availability tool may overlap. Tasks requiring a
  multi-availability tool or no tool place no such constraint.
  \item \textbf{Precedence.} If task $i$ precedes task $j$, then $j$
  may not start until $i$ completes.
  \item \textbf{Per-axis machine-configuration compatibility.} Each
  task declares a value (possibly $\emptyset$) on each of the eight
  configuration axes. For each axis independently, two concurrent
  tasks must either share the same value or at least one must be
  empty on that axis. A conflict on any axis forbids overlap.
\end{enumerate}

These constraints model reconfiguration between sequential tasks as
instantaneous; switching costs across non-overlapping configurations
are out of scope for the present formulation.

\subsubsection{Objective}

Minimise the makespan in calendar days, defined as the day index of
the latest-completing task (the number of calendar days the machine
is out of service). This is standard makespan minimisation for
RCPSP, with the working-day discretisation matching how downtime is
reported in the field.

\subsection{Candidate Algorithms}

All three algorithms solve the same scheduling task, with identical
inputs and identical output structures.

\subsubsection{Candidate A: Baseline Priority-Rule Greedy}

A priority-rule greedy scheduler maintains the set of schedulable
tasks (those whose predecessors have all been placed), selects one
by a priority rule $\pi$, assigns its earliest feasible start time,
and advances. Feasibility is checked at hourly start offsets within
the shift hours of each day, walking days in input order until a
placement satisfying all six constraints is found. Two priority rules are evaluated: \textbf{Most Total Successors
(MTS)}, which prioritises tasks with the largest count of direct
and indirect successors, and \textbf{Shortest Processing Time
(SPT)}, which prioritises tasks with the shortest duration. With
$\pi(i)$ as a generic priority, MTS sets $\pi(i)$ to the size of
the successor closure of $i$ and SPT sets $\pi(i) = -d_i$, so that
$\arg\max$ in Algorithm~\ref{alg:greedy} selects the MTS-largest or
SPT-shortest task as required. Ties break by ascending task identifier
(lexicographic on the string ID), ensuring deterministic output. The
horizon is assumed long enough that every task admits a feasible
placement; the safety-net horizon used throughout is described in
Section~\ref{sec:shiftconfig}. The full procedure is given in
Algorithm~\ref{alg:greedy}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Priority-rule greedy scheduler (Candidate A).}
\label{alg:greedy}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Task set $T$, precedence edges $E$, priority rule $\pi$
\STATE $P \gets \emptyset$ \COMMENT{placed tasks}
\STATE $S \gets \{i \in T : i \text{ has no predecessors}\}$
\WHILE{$S \neq \emptyset$}
  \STATE $i^{*} \gets \arg\max_{i \in S}\, \pi(i)$, ties broken by ascending task identifier (lexicographic on the string ID)
  \STATE $t_{i^{*}} \gets$ earliest hourly start, in input day order, where $i^{*}$ is feasible under all six constraints
  \STATE $P \gets P \cup \{i^{*}\}$;\quad $S \gets S \setminus \{i^{*}\}$
  \STATE $S \gets S \cup \{j \notin P \cup S : \text{all predecessors of } j \text{ are in } P\}$
\ENDWHILE
\RETURN start times $\{t_i\}_{i \in P}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Candidate B: Constraint Programming with CP-SAT}

The exact baseline uses Google OR-Tools CP-SAT~\cite{ortools}. Each
task is modelled as an interval variable (a CP-SAT primitive with
start, end, and duration fields) with fixed duration; its start-time
decision variable's domain contains only the start times where the
task fits inside the shift hours of a single day. The horizon $H$ is
the total length of the shift schedule in hours ($H = 24 |D|$ for
$|D|$ scheduled days); the grid spans the full 24 hours of each day,
with non-shift hours appearing as fixed zero-capacity windows so the
cumulative constraint can encode them as $C(t)=0$ runs (see the
deficit-interval construction below).

Let $C(t)$ denote the instantaneous shift capacity at time $t$ (zero
if $t$ lies outside any shift) and $C^{*} = \max_{t \in [0,H]} C(t)$
the peak shift capacity. The staffing constraint is a cumulative
constraint~\cite{vilim2015fastcumulative} on $[0,H]$ sized to $C^{*}$.
To model time-varying capacity, the horizon is partitioned into
maximal runs of constant $C(t)$; each run with $C(t) < C^{*}$
contributes a fixed-position \emph{deficit interval} of demand
$C^{*} - C(t)$ to the cumulative. The
cumulative therefore sees a constant-capacity profile while still
enforcing $\sum_i f_i \cdot \mathbb{1}[t \in [t_i, t_i + d_i)] \leq
C(t)$.

Spatial and tool exclusivity become no-overlap
constraints~\cite{laborie2008complete} (which forbid any two
intervals in a group from running concurrently) over zone-grouped
and tool-grouped intervals. Precedence is a linear inequality $t_j
\geq t_i + d_i$ per edge. Machine-configuration constraints add a
no-overlap between any two tasks with different declared values on a
shared axis; on each axis, tasks declaring $\emptyset$ on that axis
are unconstrained on that axis only.

The objective is the makespan in calendar days. The solver runs
synchronously with a user-supplied wall-clock time limit (600 seconds
in our experiments); if it terminates inside the limit the solution
is provably optimal, otherwise the best feasible solution is returned
along with a lower bound.

\subsubsection{Candidate C: Domain-Enhanced Greedy Heuristic (DEH)}

Candidate C uses the same incremental construction framework as
Candidate A but replaces the generic priority rule with a composite
scoring function. Classical priority rules order tasks using only
graph-structural and duration-structural information (successor
counts, processing times). The per-axis machine-configuration
constraint is a distinctive extension of the formulation, but
Algorithm A engages with it only at the placement-feasibility check,
never in the ordering itself. This
can place a configuration-conflicting task at the back of a day when
a configuration-compatible alternative was available, forcing more
day-breaks downstream. A domain-aware ordering signal that addresses
the per-axis constraint therefore needs to coexist with a classical
signal in the same priority computation, and the relative weight
between them is a tuning parameter rather than a derivable constant,
so we expose it and characterise its effect empirically. The
composite takes the form of two ordering signals: a critical-path
remaining term (CPR), a duration-weighted analogue of MTS, and a
continuous machine-configuration affinity term (MCA) that addresses
the per-axis constraint and has no counterpart in classical RCPSP
priority rules. A third additive term, constraint tightness (CST),
captures static schedule-state-independent task hardness and is
included as a separate tunable signal whose contribution is
characterised empirically rather than assumed.

\paragraph{Critical Path Remaining}
Before scheduling, the longest-path distance from every task to a
terminal node of the precedence graph is computed, measured in
cumulative processing duration:
\begin{equation}
\phi_{\text{cpr}}(i) = d_i + \max_{(i,j) \in E} \phi_{\text{cpr}}(j),
\end{equation}
with $\phi_{\text{cpr}}(i) = d_i$ for terminal tasks. We use the
normalised form $\hat{\phi}_{\text{cpr}}(i) = \phi_{\text{cpr}}(i) /
\max_j \phi_{\text{cpr}}(j) \in [0,1]$ in the composite score below.
CPR is a duration-weighted analogue of MTS: it weights tasks by the
total duration of the longest path to a terminal rather than by
successor count, so long downstream paths dominate the priority
rather than fan-out alone.

\paragraph{Machine-Configuration Affinity}
Although constraint 6 only forbids concurrent conflicts, the machine
retains a configuration between tasks. MCA rewards a candidate task
for declaring axis values that agree with this current configuration.
The algorithm maintains, for each axis $a$ independently, the value
$p_{\text{cur}}^{(a)}$ declared by the latest-starting placed task
that declared a value on $a$. If no placed task has yet declared a value on $a$,
$p_{\text{cur}}^{(a)} = \emptyset$ (``unset''). For a candidate task $i$ declaring a non-empty set of
axes $A_i$, the MCA score is the fraction of those axes whose
declared value $p_i^{(a)}$ either matches $p_{\text{cur}}^{(a)}$ or
for which the axis is still unset:
\begin{equation}
\phi_{\text{mca}}(i) =
\frac{1}{|A_i|} \sum_{a \in A_i}
\mathbb{1}\!\left[\, p_i^{(a)} = p_{\text{cur}}^{(a)} \,\lor\,
                       p_{\text{cur}}^{(a)} = \emptyset \,\right].
\end{equation}
Tasks declaring no axes are configuration-neutral and score
$\phi_{\text{mca}}(i) = 1$. This component addresses a constraint
type with no counterpart in classical RCPSP and therefore absent
from all standard priority rules.

\paragraph{Constraint Tightness}
CST is a static, per-task measure of placement difficulty:
\begin{equation}
\phi_{\text{cst}}(i) = \frac{1}{3} \left(
  \frac{|A_i|}{\max_j |A_j|} +
  \frac{f_i}{\max_j f_j} +
  \frac{d_i}{\max_j d_j}
\right),
\end{equation}
where each feature is normalised by the task set's own maximum (a term with a zero maximum contributes 0). The
three features are the per-task quantities that most directly drive
per-step infeasibility risk: axis-rich tasks restrict
configuration-compatible partners, staff-heavy tasks consume shift
capacity, and long-duration tasks foreclose late-day slots. Tasks
heavy on all three score near 1. The value of CST as an ordering
signal is not theoretically determined: a static hardness boost may
pull structurally hard tasks toward the front of the schedulable
pool before their placement windows fragment, or it may overrule
CPR where the CPR-implied ordering was already correct.
CST is therefore introduced as a tunable exploration parameter rather
than a load-bearing component, with its empirical contribution
characterised in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\paragraph{Composite Score}
The composite score is a convex combination of CPR and MCA,
optionally augmented by the CST term:
\begin{equation}
\text{score}(i) = (1 - w) \cdot \hat{\phi}_{\text{cpr}}(i)
                + w \cdot \phi_{\text{mca}}(i)
                + \gamma \cdot \phi_{\text{cst}}(i),
\label{eq:composite}
\end{equation}
where $w \in [0,1]$ controls the trade-off between critical-path
priority and configuration affinity, and $\gamma \geq 0$ controls the
CST boost. The convex form keeps CPR and MCA on the same $[0,1]$
scale so that $w$ has a direct interpretation as the fractional
emphasis placed on configuration affinity. CST is an additive term
rather than a third component of the convex combination so that
varying $\gamma$ at fixed $w$ isolates the contribution of static
hardness from the CPR/MCA trade-off (the CST ablation is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:cross-instance}). Scores order tasks; the
highest-scoring task is selected at each step and assigned its
earliest feasible start time. Ties break by ascending task
identifier (lexicographic on the string ID).
When $w = 0$ and $\gamma = 0$, the algorithm is pure CPR (the
duration-weighted analogue of MTS); when $w = 1$ and $\gamma = 0$,
it is pure configuration affinity, a signal classical priority rules
cannot express.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Instances}
\label{sec:instances}

The study uses real industrial RCPSP instances drawn from distinct
machine modules. Each instance is supplied as a three-table dataset
(task properties, a precedence matrix, and a spatial-zone matrix)
used internally by the industrial partner. Task
identifiers, tool names, and zone labels are masked at source before
the data leaves the company; numeric attributes are unmasked, so the
algorithmic quantities reported here are unaffected by the masking.

Four instances are evaluated, MOD\_A, MOD\_B, MOD\_C, and MOD\_D, chosen
to span complementary difficulty profiles: a small dense module
(MOD\_C), a large constraint-rich module (MOD\_A), a shallow-DAG
module with a single-availability tool (MOD\_B), and a deep dense
precedence-driven module with minimal axis activity (MOD\_D). The
intent is not to characterise every conceivable instance but to
exercise the algorithmic comparison against complementary regions of
the design space rather than against repeated draws from a single
profile. Table~\ref{tab:instances} summarises their structural

\subsection{Shift Configuration}
\label{sec:shiftconfig}

For consistency across instances, all are evaluated under the same
shift configuration: a single 14-hour shift per calendar day staffed
by 6 engineers, repeated for 30 calendar days. The 30-day horizon
is set as a safety-net upper bound, chosen generously so that the
horizon does not artificially constrain the schedule on the
instances at hand.

\subsection{Methodology and Metrics}
\label{sec:methodology}

For each instance, fifteen scheduling runs are conducted: one each
of MTS and SPT; ten DEH runs covering the $5 \times 2$ grid of
weights $w \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0\}$ and CST settings
$\gamma \in \{0, 0.5\}$; and three independent runs of CP-SAT with
a 600-second wall-clock limit. MTS, SPT, and DEH are deterministic
given their inputs; only CP-SAT has solver-internal variance, hence
the three repeats apply only there. The grid spans both the convex
CPR/MCA combination ($\gamma = 0$) and the CST-augmented composite
($\gamma = 0.5$), so the isolated effect of CST is recoverable as a
paired difference per $(\text{instance}, w)$. The 600-second CP-SAT
budget is set as a safety-net upper bound on per-instance wall-clock
time, chosen generously so that any instance the solver fails to
close within this budget is treated as time-limited.

For each run we record success (a Boolean flag for whether the
algorithm produced a feasible schedule, with failures not retried);
makespan in calendar days; algorithm-internal runtime in milliseconds,
excluding I/O, validation, and result formatting; best bound and
proved status for CP-SAT only; and the optimality gap, computed
after all runs complete, as $(m_{\text{algo}} - m^{*}) / m^{*}$, where $m^{*}$ is
CP-SAT's best feasible makespan on the instance (the proved optimum
where CP-SAT terminates within the 600\,s budget, the best incumbent
at the cap otherwise). On any instance where CP-SAT does not prove
optimality within the budget, DEH's gap against CP-SAT's proved
lower bound is also reported as a conservative cross-check
(Section~\ref{sec:per-instance-results}).

To characterise the speed--quality trade-off directly, a CP-SAT
budget sweep is conducted alongside the headline 600-second runs:
one additional CP-SAT run per $(\text{instance}, \tau)$ pair across
the five-point logarithmic grid $\tau \in \{0.1, 1, 10, 60, 600\}$
seconds, independent of the three headline runs at $\tau = 600$\,s.
The heuristic baseline is not re-run, since DEH at the recommended
$(w=0, \gamma=0)$ configuration completes in under 100 ms on every
instance, so its existing measurement covers every budget at or above
its construction cost.

The CST ablation is also evaluated with a paired Wilcoxon
signed-rank test on the 20 per-$(\text{instance}, w)$ makespan deltas
($\gamma = 0.5$ minus $\gamma = 0$), with zero deltas discarded per
the standard Wilcoxon convention.

\subsection{Hypothesis Evaluation}

Throughout, ``best-configured DEH'' denotes the configuration with
the lowest mean optimality gap across the four evaluated instances,
applied identically to every instance; on the present set this is
$(w, \gamma) = (0, 0)$. The choice is at the cross-instance grain
rather than per-instance, but it remains an in-sample selection
across the four instances. A stronger pre-registration would have
fixed the configuration before any evaluation runs and is left for
the larger instance set identified as future work.

H1 is evaluated by comparing the mean optimality gap of
best-configured DEH across the four instances against the
mean gaps of MTS and SPT. H2 is evaluated by checking that
this mean gap does not exceed 15\%. The CST design ablation
($\gamma = 0.5$ vs $\gamma = 0$ at fixed $w$, paired per instance)
is reported alongside the H1/H2 verdicts.

\subsection{Implementation}

All algorithms are implemented in pure Python and depend only on the
\texttt{ortools} library (CP-SAT, version $\geq 9.8$, $<10$) for the
CP-SAT solver. Experiments are run on an Apple M1 Pro with 16 GB of
RAM under macOS 26.3 with Python 3.13.3; all runtimes are wall-clock
medians, excluding I/O. Source code, the pinned dependency specification, raw
per-instance result CSVs, and aggregation scripts are provided as
supplementary material.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Cross-Instance Interpretation}

CP-SAT's behaviour varies sharply with instance structure. On
MOD\_A, MOD\_C, and MOD\_D it proves optimality in under five seconds
despite spanning a roughly five-fold range in task count and DAG
depth. On MOD\_B, which combines a single-availability tool
with no axis activity, a moderate-depth DAG, and a heavy zone
footprint (31 zones across 90 tasks), it exhausts the 600-second
budget without closing the gap. The solver-internal gap of roughly 18\% is stable across the three
CP-SAT runs rather than reducible by search randomisation: all three
runs converge to the same incumbent (11 days) and lower bound (9
days). The contrast directly motivates the heuristic
comparison. Where CP-SAT proves optimality cheaply, the heuristic
gap is the entire question. Where CP-SAT cannot prove optimality
within an industrially feasible budget, an inexpensive constructive
heuristic with a small known gap to incumbent is operationally
useful even without an exact reference.

Within DEH, the gap shape across $w$ at $\gamma = 0$ is informative.
Pure CPR ($w = 0$) strictly beats MTS on MOD\_A and MOD\_B and ties
on the other two instances. The full per-$w$ grid in the
supplementary CSVs shows that the per-instance gap is monotone
non-decreasing in $w$ on three of the four instances, indicating that
configuration continuity alone is a worse ordering criterion than
critical-path priority on the current set. CST is harmful at $w = 0$
on MOD\_A (adds one day; see Table~\ref{tab:h1summary}'s
$(w=0, \gamma=0.5)$ row) and neutral on the other three; the per-$w$
ablation pattern at $\gamma = 0.5$ in the supplementary CSVs is mixed
(helpful on MOD\_D at intermediate $w$, uniformly harmful on MOD\_B,
mixed on MOD\_A), and these intermediate-$w$ gains do not offset
MOD\_A's loss at the recommended $w = 0$. CST is therefore harmful
at the recommended configuration rather than merely neutral, and is
retained as a per-instance tunable signal because intermediate-$w$
benefits remain recoverable on some instances; it does not contribute
to the deployment-recommended configuration and is not load-bearing
for the headline comparison.

\subsection{Implications for Deployment Scenarios}

For \emph{scenario iteration}, the median CP-SAT runtime at the
600-second cap ranges from 0.08 s (MOD\_C) to 4.2 s (MOD\_A) on
instances it proves optimal and saturates the cap on MOD\_B. A
hundred-scenario sweep that takes seconds-to-minutes under CP-SAT on
the easy instances would take well over an hour on MOD\_B if rerun
naively, whereas DEH at $w = 0$ completes each scenario in
under 100\,ms on every instance, so the same hundred-scenario sweep
finishes in under 10\,s. In
the sub-second regime relevant for an engineer running scenario
sweeps interactively, the heuristic is therefore the only choice
that produces a usable schedule on three of the four instances. For
\emph{embedded deployment}, all DEH variants execute in well under
one second with no external solver dependency, as the scenario
requires. For
\emph{independent verification}, the agreement between DEH at
$w = 0$ and CP-SAT (5.3\% gap on MOD\_A, 9.1\% on MOD\_B, 0\% on
MOD\_C, 10\% on MOD\_D) gives a usable cross-check baseline.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

The strongest limitation is sample size. With $N = 4$ at the time of
writing, the cross-instance verdicts (H1, H2) should be read as
preliminary directional evidence rather than statistical
generalisation; adequate coverage of the RCPSP design space requires
systematic sampling across multiple structural parameter
axes~\cite{vanhoucke2008measurement}, which the present four
instances do not yet provide. Each evaluated instance is also one
module of one machine, while in deployment a lithography machine
carries multiple upgradeable modules and ASML operates many machines.
Additional modules from the ASML instance pipeline are expected to
become available over time, opening both per-machine and cross-machine
scaling; H1 and H2 can be revisited under non-parametric tests once
sufficient power is available.

MOD\_B is the only instance whose heuristic gap is reported against
an unproved incumbent; the conservative cross-check against CP-SAT's
proved lower bound is in Section~\ref{sec:per-instance-results}. Its
structural profile (heavy zone footprint, a single-availability
tool, minimal axis activity) motivates building out more instances
with similar features to test whether CP-SAT's difficulty generalises.

The CP-SAT budget sweep uses a coarse five-point logarithmic grid
and a single CP-SAT run per $(\text{instance}, \tau)$ pair; a finer
grid with multi-seed replication would tighten the curve. The MCA
component treats machine reconfiguration as instantaneous, while in
reality switching configurations consumes time; incorporating
reconfiguration costs would likely raise the value of configuration
continuity and shift the optimal $w$ upward, so the present setup
may underestimate MCA's contribution. All instances are also
evaluated under a fixed shift configuration (one 14-hour shift per
day, six staff, 30-day horizon); a horizon-and-shift sweep is a
separate future-work direction.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This thesis formalised the upgrade-scheduling problem encountered at
ASML as a Resource-Constrained Project Scheduling Problem with five
domain-specific extensions, and evaluated three algorithmic
candidates against it: a baseline priority-rule greedy (MTS,
SPT), an exact CP-SAT solver (CP-SAT), and a domain-enhanced
greedy heuristic scored by a convex combination of a critical-path
remaining term (CPR) and a continuous machine-configuration affinity
term (MCA), with an optional constraint-tightness term (CST) as a
tunable exploration (DEH).

On the four instances evaluated, CP-SAT proves optimality within
seconds on MOD\_A, MOD\_C, and MOD\_D under a 600-second budget but
exhausts that budget on MOD\_B with a non-zero solver-internal gap
(incumbent 11, lower bound 9). The recommended heuristic
configuration, DEH at $w = 0, \gamma = 0$, attains a mean
gap of 6.1\% against the CP-SAT incumbent, strictly below MTS
(9.7\%) and SPT (31.0\%), well below the pre-registered 15\%
mean-gap threshold, and a 37\% relative reduction in mean gap
versus the best classical priority rule. Pre-registered
hypotheses H1 and H2 are therefore supported. The CST design ablation
is conditionally beneficial: $\gamma = 0.5$ is equal-or-better than
$\gamma = 0$ in 14 of 20 paired comparisons, but strict losses (6)
outnumber strict wins (3) two-to-one, and at the recommended $w = 0$
setting $\gamma = 0.5$ strictly degrades the mean gap by 1.3
percentage points relative to $\gamma = 0$ (driven by a one-day loss
on MOD\_A). CST is retained as a per-instance tunable signal but is
not part of the recommended deployment configuration and is not
load-bearing for the headline comparison.

The CP-SAT budget sweep characterises the speed--quality
trade-off and reveals three regimes. At 0.1 s per instance, CP-SAT
returns no feasible solution on three of the four instances
(MOD\_A, MOD\_B, MOD\_D); only the smallest instance (MOD\_C) is
solved at this budget. The heuristic returns a feasible schedule on
every instance at this budget, so in the sub-second regime the
heuristic is the only choice that yields a usable answer on three
of the four instances. At one second, CP-SAT matches the heuristic
on MOD\_B, MOD\_C, and MOD\_D and beats it by one day on MOD\_A;
MOD\_C is proved optimal at this budget while the other three
remain unproved incumbents.
From ten seconds onward, CP-SAT beats the heuristic by one day on
three of the four instances and ties on the fourth. The heuristic is
therefore not uniformly best: it dominates in the sub-second regime,
while CP-SAT weakly dominates from one second of compute upward when
an unproved-but-high-quality incumbent is acceptable.

A domain-enhanced greedy heuristic
can close a substantial fraction of the residual gap that classical
priority rules leave against CP-SAT, a 37\% relative reduction in
mean gap versus MTS and a mean gap well below the 15\%
pre-registered threshold, at sub-second cost. CP-SAT remains the
gold-standard reference at multi-second budgets, where it matches or
beats the heuristic on every instance evaluated. The heuristic's
operational value covers the three deployment scenarios identified
at the outset: it dominates the sub-second regime as the only
candidate that returns a feasible schedule on three of the four
instances; it removes the external-solver dependency for embedded
deployment; and at modest gap it serves as an independent
cross-check baseline. The verdict is preliminary at $N = 4$; further
industrial instances, finer-grained budget sweeps, and multi-seed
CP-SAT replication are the next refinements.
